Design language

Personal Tracker is a quiet, honest instrument panel that belongs to one person. It has the precision of a good production tool and the personality of a room someone actually lives in. Those two things are usually opposites. Holding them together is the whole design brief.

Try the five themes — top right. Everything on this page is built from the tokens it documents.

This document is not a picture of the design system. It is the design system, running. If a rule here cannot be demonstrated on this page, it is not a rule yet.

The tension, stated plainly

Most tools you admire are precise but anonymous. They are built to look correct in a screenshot on someone else's laptop, so every decision trends toward the safe middle: one grey, one blue, one radius, no texture, no jokes, no risk. That discipline is why they feel good to use. It is also why they all feel the same.

The old web had the opposite problem. Every page was personal but incoherent — your own colours, your own cursor, your own tiled background, and no craft holding it together.

This app takes the craft from the first and the ownership from the second. Rigorous grid, rigorous contrast, rigorous motion — and then, inside that frame, real colour, real texture, and a handful of ideas a large product would never ship because they cannot be justified to a committee. This is one person's dashboard. It does not need to be justified to a committee.

The eight laws

These are the product's character, not style preferences. They are what makes four unrelated workspaces feel like one place. Every other section of this document is these laws applied to a surface.

1
A quiet day looks quiet.

An empty section is not rendered — no "0 items", no empty card, no skeleton of nothing. Sections appear because they have something to say. A wholly empty page gets a real empty state, and that empty state is allowed to be beautiful.

2
Never lie by omission.

If a source failed, say so in place. A short list and a broken list look identical, and that is the worst failure this app can have. Stale mirror, mid-flight sync, a statistic too young to mean anything — the page says it, where it happened.

3
Never invent precision.

A task due "Tuesday" shows "Tuesday", not "Tuesday 00:00". A role never matched shows nothing, not "0/0". No prior period shows "No prior period to compare", not "0%".

4
Colour is a claim.

Green means money came back — never spending, never generic success. The pipeline hues mean one stage each and appear nowhere else. Workspace accent means "you are here". If you need a colour and none of the meanings is true, use ink and a shape.

5
View state lives in the URL.

Filters, search, sort, grouping, tabs, pagination — query parameters, all of them. A narrowed view survives a refresh, the back button, and being pasted into a note. Component state is for the genuinely ephemeral: an open menu, a confirm step, an unsent draft.

6
It works before JavaScript does.

Search and filters are GET forms and links. Server components render data. Client components exist for interaction, not fetching. A slow connection degrades to a working page, not a spinner.

7
Personality lives in the chrome, never in the data.

Texture, colour, whimsy and motion belong to the frame: nav, empty states, marks, themes, transitions. The numbers themselves are set plainly, in tabular figures, on a clean ground. Decorate the room, not the instruments.

8
Reasoning goes in the code.

Every non-obvious decision carries a comment saying why, in the file where it lives. This codebase is unusually good at that and it must stay that way — it is the only thing stopping the next contributor from re-deriving a decision badly.

Personality, and the contract that keeps it usable

Law 7 is the one doing the load-bearing work, so it gets its own section. Whimsy in a data tool fails when it competes with the data. It succeeds when it occupies the space around the data that would otherwise be dead air.

Where personality is welcome

  • The frame: top bar, workspace marks, the switcher, focus rings, the status line.
  • The ground: texture, theme, canvas colour.
  • Empty and finished states — the moments where there is genuinely nothing to read.
  • Transitions between states, and the feel of a control under the hand.
  • Anything the user explicitly turned on.

Where it is forbidden

  • Anywhere a number, date, amount or status is being read.
  • Any surface where the user is deciding something — a review queue, a confirm, a form.
  • Anything that delays the user by even 100ms to be charming.
  • Anything a person cannot turn off if it stops being charming on the four-hundredth viewing.
Do

A hand-drawn underline under the active nav item — chrome, static, costs nothing, and is different in every workspace.

A generative sigil on the "nothing needs you today" state — a moment that is otherwise empty.

Don't

A wobble on the spend figure. That figure is the reason the page exists; it gets tabular numerals and nothing else.

A confetti burst on completing a task. Fine once; it is contempt by the fiftieth time.

The one-second rule. Any flourish must be over in under a second, must never block input, and must vanish entirely under prefers-reduced-motion. If it cannot meet all three, it is not a flourish, it is an obstacle.

Colour

Every colour is a token in app/globals.css, and every token is defined twice — once per theme. Never write a raw Tailwind palette class. No bg-emerald-50, no text-amber-700, no border-red-200: a raw palette colour is a colour that cannot follow the theme, and it will be wrong in four of the five themes the moment it is written.

The ground and the ink

Four grounds and three inks. That is the entire neutral system, and it is enough because depth comes from a hairline border, not from a shadow.

The three-ink rule. ink is what the row is. ink-muted is everything that supports it — and it is the only grey allowed to carry information you would miss. ink-ghost is decoration: placeholders, disabled controls, redundant glyphs. A count, a timestamp, a table header or a hint is information, and takes ink-muted. There is no third informational grey, because an ink-faint that clears 4.5:1 is ink-muted with extra steps — which is what the measurements said when the old one was fixed rather than retired.

Workspace accents — the app changes colour with the room

This is the single most visible personal touch, and the one a large product would refuse. Each workspace owns a hue. It tints the nav's active state, the rail, the focus ring, links and the workspace mark — so you know where you are peripherally, before you read a word. Switching workspace does not just change the content; it changes the light in the room.

The four are stops on one arc — sky, violet, fuchsia, rose — with indigo as the app's own accent at the blue end. Drawing them from a single sweep rather than from around the wheel is what keeps four differently-coloured workspaces reading as one product instead of four products sharing a login. Colour is reinforcement, never the identifier: the glyph tells you which workspace you are in and the hue confirms it, which is also why two adjacent stops being similar is not a problem.

How it works mechanically

/* The shell sets one variable. Nothing below it knows or cares. */

/* Components only ever reference --accent. Never --w-shopping directly. */
.nav-item[aria-current] { background: var(--accent-tint); color: var(--accent); }

Chrome takes the accent; content takes its own meaning. The jobs workspace is violet — but a pipeline card is still submitted-blue, because that colour is a claim about the pursuit, not about where you are standing. When accent and semantic colour meet, the semantic one wins and the accent retreats to the frame. This is the rule that stops per-workspace colour becoming per-workspace chaos.

Gradients and marks

One hue, or none. A gradient may move at most ~20% in lightness within a single hue. Never blend two hues — an orange-to-pink diagonal ramp is decoration pretending to be identity, it is the first thing that looks cheap when the theme changes, and it makes four workspace marks that are only distinguishable if you already know which is which. The single exception is the home mark's key, below, and it is an exception because that ramp is the product's identity rather than a decoration of it.

Four squares. Three never change; the fourth is the module. The constant three are monochrome and carry no colour at all. The top-right one is a different shape and a different hue, and it is the only colour anywhere in the mark.

That split is the whole system. The constant three say this is the same app without being looked at; the key says which room without being read. And it does not run out — the version before this encoded the module in which of four nodes was promoted, which was legible and capped at exactly four modules. A shape plus a hue goes as far as anyone sensibly takes this.

What it costsBelow roughly 32px the fine detail closes up and the key becomes a coloured blob. That is the honest trade: at that size no picture is legible and the hue is doing the work anyway. Draw for 32px and up; check that 24px still reads as a colour in the corner.
HexesFixed, not themed. A mark is an object, and an app icon does not invert when the OS goes dark.

The home key is the one place two hues may meet. Everywhere else a gradient stays inside a single hue, because a two-hue ramp is decoration pretending to be identity. Here it is the identity: blue into pink is what this product has used for itself since before it had modules, and the home mark is the only thing entitled to wear it.

The trade this makes: identity is carried by shape and hue rather than by a picture of a thing. It works because a mark in this app always sits beside its own label — the switcher, the home tiles, Account, the mobile top bar. It would not work on a browser tab.

Icons

  • Lucide, 1.75 stroke, currentColor, never filled. One library, one weight, no exceptions.
  • Never an emoji as an interface icon. Emoji render differently on every platform, cannot take a colour, cannot take a stroke weight, and carry a tone this app does not have. They are fine inside a user's own note text; they are never in the interface.
  • 16px in rows and buttons, 14px inside a badge or chip, 20px+ only in an empty state.
  • A decorative icon takes aria-hidden. An icon that is the control takes an accessible name.

Themes

A theme is a small, closed set of variables — four grounds, three inks, two borders, an accent family, four workspace hues, a texture and its opacity. Nothing else. That constraint is what makes a theme something a person could plausibly write themselves, which is the entire point.

Mechanically: the raw values are declared per theme as --c-* variables, and an @theme inline block maps them onto Tailwind's names. That indirection is the whole trick — bg-surface compiles to background-color: var(--c-surface) rather than baking one theme's hex into the stylesheet, so one class follows five themes.

Tell tailwind-merge about every named scale you add, in lib/cn.ts. A size and a colour are both spelled text-*; left untold, it treated text-surface and text-body as the same kind of class and silently deleted the first — which is how the primary button lost its label colour and rendered invisible in three of the five themes. There is a test for it. 

/* The whole contract. Anything not in this list is derived. */
[data-theme="yours"] {
  color-scheme: light | dark;
  --canvas --surface --raised --sunken
  --border --border-strong
  --ink --ink-muted --ink-faint
  --accent --accent-hover --accent-tint
  --w-shopping --w-jobs --w-todo --w-vault  (+ their tints)
  --positive --caution --danger             (+ their tints)
  --texture --grain-opacity --grain-blend
  --font-ui --font-display --font-mono      (optional)
}

Rules

  • Themes are named, not numbered. "Riso", not "Theme 3". A named thing is a thing you choose; a numbered thing is a setting you tolerate.
  • Default follows the system (prefers-color-scheme) until the user picks. Once they pick, that choice sticks and the system preference stops mattering.
  • The choice lives on core.account_settings, so it follows the account across devices — and is mirrored into a cookie so the server renders the right theme on first paint. A theme that flashes white before going dark is worse than no dark mode.
  • Every theme must pass the same contrast gate. There is no "expressive theme" exemption. Riso is louder and passes.
  • The picker previews live on hover — you see the room before you move into it.
  • Lightbox is the one theme with a resting shadow, and it is the exception that proves the rule. Its cards are objects on a bench, so --sheet-edge gives every sheet a 1px lit top edge, a bench-coloured outline in place of the grey hairline, a deep soft drop and a faint bloom. The token is none in the other four; nothing outside globals.css knows.
  • Density is a second dial in the same panel. Comfortable · Snug · Dense sets data-density on <html>, which drives --card-p and --row-y. Per device, in a cookie: a laptop and a phone want different answers.

Texture and material

One fixed, non-interactive grain layer over the whole viewport. It is the difference between a surface and a void, and it costs a single ::before with an inline SVG — no image request, no layout, no repaint. 

body::before {
  content: ""; position: fixed; inset: 0;
  z-index: 9999; pointer-events: none;          /* never intercepts a click */
  background-image: var(--texture);
  opacity: var(--grain-opacity);                 /* 3–11%, per theme */
  mix-blend-mode: var(--grain-blend);            /* multiply light, overlay dark */
}
  • 6% on the neutral themes, 7% on the Lightbox bench, 9% on Riso, where it is the point. It was 3.5% and did not survive a 1x screenshot, which means it was not doing its job on most monitors. Above ~12% it stops reading as material and starts reading as a rendering bug.
  • Riso's texture is a halftone dot, not noise. A printed theme with no printing in it was the doc and the app disagreeing; the app now carries the 6px dot on --grain in its own block.
  • pointer-events: none is not optional — a full-viewport overlay that swallows clicks is a catastrophic, hard-to-diagnose bug.
  • The texture never sits between the user and text they are reading closely. Long-form prose — a vault note — lifts onto --surface, which the grain sits over at a lower effective contrast because the surface is lighter. If it ever reduces legibility, the grain loses.
  • No texture inside charts, tables, or any element the user is measuring by eye.

Other materials, used sparingly

  • Inset accent bar — box-shadow: inset 3px 0 0 {colour} on a thumbnail or row. Already in the inventory row and it is one of the best details in the app.
  • Keycap borders — a 2px bottom border on the keyboard-hint chips, so they read as physical. ⌘ K
  • Paper offset shadow — a hard, un-blurred 2px offset shadow, only on things pretending to be paper (marginalia). Never on cards.

Typography

Three families. Inter does all the work. Instrument Serif is the voice — page titles and the one big figure, and nothing else. A mono for the status line, keycaps, and anything that is literally a machine talking.

You already own two of these.

The display face is where the app stops sounding like software. Use it big or not at all — at 13px a grotesque is just a slightly odd Inter, and the difference is noise rather than voice. No serifs anywhere: this is an instrument panel, not a document.

Numbers

Anything in a column of numbers takes .tabular. Money, counts, dates, percentages, ages, durations. A column of proportional digits that shifts as it updates is the cheapest possible way to make a dashboard feel unreliable. Money is always formatted through lib/money.ts — never an inline toFixed.

Two borders, and the difference is legal rather than aesthetic. border is the container hairline. control is the border of an input, a select or a checkbox — often the only thing identifying it as a control, which makes it a user-interface component owing 3:1 under WCAG 1.4.11 that a hairline does not pay.

No shadow at rest. Depth is a 1px border on a surface over a canvas. Shadow exists only for things genuinely floating — menus and popovers — and transiently on lift hover. A card with a resting drop shadow is wrong in every theme, and in the dark themes it is invisible as well as wrong.

The shell

Top bar

One component, <WorkspaceNav>, configured per module. There must never again be four near-identical copies — that is how Todo lost its notifications button and Jobs ended up a pixel smaller than everyone else. 

<WorkspaceNav

Left to right, and this order is fixed:

  1. Workspace mark and switcher — which workspace, and the way out.
  2. Section navigation — where in it.
  3. Notifications, feedback — cross-cutting, identical everywhere.
  4. Module settings (gear), then account (avatar).

Two settings entries, and the line is fixed: the gear is this module's settings; the avatar is the account's. A setting belongs to the account if it would still mean anything with the module switched off — your name, timezone, display currency, theme. Everything else belongs to the module it is about.

Section nav rules

  • Pill. Active is bg-accent-tint text-accent; rest text-ink-muted with hover:bg-sunken hover:text-ink. Always text-ui (13px) — never text-sm.
  • aria-current="page" on the active item. Always.
  • Prefix match by default; exact where an href is a parent of the others (/todo, /vault).
  • A badge only where an unattended count causes silent data damage. Review has earned one — an unworked queue is how the dashboard quietly becomes wrong. Nothing else has. Badges are caution-fill with dark ink, never white on orange (2.3:1).
  • Eight sections is the ceiling. Past that, group them or move something into module settings. Jobs has ten and it is the app's worst surface on a phone.
  • On overflow: horizontal scroll, an edge fade, and scrollIntoView({ block:'nearest', inline:'center' }) on the active item at mount. A user must never land on a page whose own tab is off-screen with no cue that it exists.

The sidebar

Sections are a vertical list, not a horizontal strip. A strip stops working at about six items and the job search has ten; a column holds ten without crowding, has room for a count beside each, and frees the top bar to do the one thing a top bar is good at — naming the page and holding what belongs to the account rather than to the page.

  • Left to right, top to bottom, and this order is fixed: the switcher at the top of the sidebar, sections under it; then in the top bar, the page's name, and the account-level controls at the right.
  • The active section is a ground, not a tint. A tint would have to be legible on five different shells; a 2px bar in the workspace mark's own fixed hue is vivid on all of them, so the active row gets bg-shell-hover plus that bar.
  • The shell has its own inks. --c-shell, --c-shell-ink, --c-shell-muted, --c-shell-border, --c-shell-hover. In four themes the shell is a shade of the surface and these barely differ; in Lightbox the shell is near-black under a lit page, which is the whole reason they exist.
  • Below lg the sidebar is a drawer, opened from a button in the top bar and closed by opening anything in it.
  • The filter rail stays a second column from xl up, and a sheet below. Merging it into the sidebar is the obvious next move and is deliberately not done yet: the rail is server-rendered markup inside the page, and hoisting it into a layout means either a portal — which would make filters appear only after hydration, breaking law 6 — or threading it through every page's props.
  • The top bar names the page, and falls back to the workspace rather than to nothing. A bar that goes blank on an unlisted route reads as broken.

The workspace mark and switcher

The most-repeated navigation in a four-workspace app, so it gets real attention. It is one mark, one label, one chevron — one hit target. Home is the first row of the menu, not a second mystery square beside the first.

The mark: workspace gradient as field, workspace glyph knocked out

In the real component the glyph is the module's Lucide icon in white, not an emoji. The gradient alone was decoration — three of the four current gradients start on the same blue, so a hue is not something a person can identify a workspace by. The glyph is the mnemonic; the gradient is the mood.

One icon language per module, everywhere. Switcher, home tiles and the Account workspace toggles all use the same Lucide glyph for a given module. Glyph, gradient, accent, label and description all live in lib/modules.ts and are read from there — that list exists precisely so a second list cannot drift out of sync with it.

The menu

  • Every row carries a live count — "6 open pursuits", "3 due today", "2 to review". The switcher is the only surface that can answer "is anything happening in the workspaces I am not looking at", so it should. Those figures already exist on the home page; extract one loadModuleCounts() and share it.
  • Rows: mark, label, description, count, check on the current one.
  • The current module is always listed, even when switched off in Account. You can only be there by URL, and a menu that will not admit where you are is worse than one showing a hidden module.
  • Switching returns you where you were in that workspace, not to its home, when a last path is known.
  • Keyboard: ↑↓ move, Home/End jump, Enter activates, Esc closes and returns focus to the trigger. Roving tabindex — a role="menu" that is really a list of tab stops lies to a screen reader.
  • ⌘K opens it. ⌘1–4 jump straight to a workspace.

Notifications, feedback, account

The bell only exists once it has something to say. A permanently empty notifications panel teaches people to stop looking, and by the time the first real notification arrives nobody will see it. Either it is wired to real events or it is not rendered. It is currently furniture on three of four navs.

What belongs in it — all of these already happen, and are currently shouted as full-width page banners instead:

  • A Gmail sync finished, and what it found.
  • A return window closes in n days.
  • An interview is tomorrow and has no prep notes.
  • The vault token expired and nothing has mirrored since a date.
  • The review queue crossed a threshold.

A notification is one line, written as the thing that happened, optionally a detail line and a link to where it happened. Not "You have 1 new notification" — "Your Amazon return window closes Friday".

Popover contract

  • Trigger: 32px round icon button, aria-expanded, aria-haspopup="dialog", active state bg-accent-tint text-accent.
  • Panel: rounded-card border bg-raised shadow-lg.
  • Below sm it pins to the viewport (fixed inset-x-4 top-16), not to the trigger. Anchored to a right-hand button it hangs off the left edge of a phone, where nothing can scroll it back and Safari answers a focused field by zooming the whole page out to reach it.
  • Closes on Esc and outside click; the outside-click listener attaches on a deferred tick so the opening click does not immediately close it.
  • Focus moves into the panel on open, is trapped while open, and returns to the trigger on close. aria-modal="true". This is the part currently missing everywhere.

Account

Row rules

  • The whole row is the link. Use a stretched pseudo-element (<span class="absolute inset-0">) so the hit area is the row, not the six characters of the title.
  • Supporting facts join with · and truncate as one line. Never wrap a metadata line to two.
  • Hover-reveal on pointer, always-visible on touch: opacity-100 sm:opacity-0 sm:group-hover:opacity-100 sm:group-focus-within:opacity-100. A row whose actions never appear is a row a phone user cannot act on. This pattern exists in task-row.tsx and should be everywhere.
  • Destructive row actions use an inline two-step confirm, never window.confirm — it is blocked in enough contexts to be unreliable, and it is ugly in every theme.

Table rules

Use <Table> from components/ui/table.tsx. There were eight hand-built tables that disagreed on header casing, on whether a row lit up, and on which sliver of a row was the link. The primitive holds every rule below so no file has to. 

<Table flush>                              // flush: inside a padded card
  <THead><TR><TH>Item</TH><TH num>Total</TH></TR></THead>
  <TBody>
    <TR href="/shopping/orders/…">         // the whole row goes here
      <TD primary>Sony WH-1000XM5</TD>    // carries the link, stretched over the row
      <TD num label="Total">$348.00</TD>   // label: what the cell is called on a phone
    </TR>
  </TBody>

  • Headers: text-micro, uppercase, tracking-wider, ink-muted.
  • Sortable headers are links that write ?sort=, with the active one in accent and an arrow. If a table has more than ten rows it is sortable.
  • Numeric columns right-aligned and tabular. Always. (TH num, TD num.)
  • A row with one destination goes there from anywhere in the row (TR href + TD primary), and says so under the cursor.
  • Below md, every row stacks into label/value pairs and the header row goes. Do not ship a five-column table to a phone and call the horizontal scroll a design. A table that is genuinely a grid — a calendar — sets stack={false} and gets the right-edge fade instead.

States

Empty

The app's existing empty states are its best feature and the pattern is non-negotiable: every empty state branches on whether a filter is applied, and each branch gets its own copy and its own action.

No matching items

Nothing matched that search across names, tags and categories. You probably don't own it.

  • Filtered empty → "No matching items" + Clear filters. Never suggest creating something when they were searching for something.
  • Genuinely empty → say what will fill it and offer the action that does so.
  • Finished empty → this is a reward, not an absence. "Nothing needs you today." It gets the generative sigil (see Alive) and a quiet way back to the data.

Loading

Skeletons, never spinners — and there are currently neither. The app has zero loading.tsx files, zero <Suspense> boundaries and exactly one use of the .skeleton utility, while every page performs four to seven sequential database round-trips before rendering anything. This is the largest perceived-performance gap in the product.

  • Every route group gets a loading.tsx that renders the page's real shape in skeletons — the header, the rail, three rows. Not a centred spinner.
  • Wrap slow, independent regions in <Suspense> so a slow chart does not hold the whole page.
  • A skeleton that appears for under ~200ms is a flicker. Delay it.

Error, and honest failure

  • Every route group gets an error.tsx. There are currently none — inventory/page.tsx throws straight into the framework's default screen.
  • A partial failure is stated in place and never silently swallowed. The agenda's "Job search could not be read just now, so anything from it is missing from this page" is the model for the whole app.
  • An error message says what could not be done, not what the exception was. If there is a retry, it is a button.

Forms and buttons

  • One primary per view. If two things look equally primary, neither is.
  • primary = accent fill. secondary = surface + border. ghost = the third-most-important thing on a row. danger = destructive only, and never the primary of a form.
  • Sizes: sm in rows and headers, md in forms, lg only on marketing and onboarding.
  • A button that triggers async work shows its own pending label ("Saving…") and disables. It never leaves the user guessing whether the click landed.

Fields

  • text-base sm:text-sm on every input. 16px on a phone is what stops iOS zooming the page on focus; anything smaller is a bug.
  • One <Field> composing label, control, hint and error — hints are currently hand-rolled text-[11px] text-ink-faint in dozens of places, which is both off-scale and under contrast.
  • Errors are inline, under the field, in danger. Form-level results use the Banner component, not a bespoke coloured paragraph.
  • Search boxes are GET forms. Debounced client-side search is an enhancement layered on a working form, never a replacement for one.

Interaction

Everything above describes how the app looks. This describes how it behaves, which is the half that decides whether it is actually good to use.

The four loops

Every workspace runs some subset of the same four-beat loop. Naming them is what stops a page from quietly becoming the wrong kind of page — the failure that turns an agenda into an archive, and a review queue into a list nobody works.

BeatThe questionDesign obligation
Capture"Get this out of my head / inbox."Must cost almost nothing. One field, always reachable, never a page transition. Ingestion counts as capture: mail arriving is the user capturing.
Triage"Is this real, and is it mine?"A queue with a keyboard rhythm and a default. Every item leaves the queue in one keystroke. An unworked triage queue is how the data quietly becomes wrong — this is the beat the app most often skips.
Act"Do the thing."The action is on the row. Nothing that can be done from a list should require opening a detail page.
Review"Is this working?"Aggregates, cohorts, funnels. Honest about sample size and about what is too young to mean anything.

Every loop needs an entry point at most one click from anywhere in its workspace, and the page that answers "what do I do" is never the same page as the one that answers "what is going on". /jobs/today and /todo get this exactly right and are the model. /shopping has no equivalent — its dashboard answers "what is going on" and nothing answers "what do I do", which is why the returns deadline is the thing people miss.

Where am I, and how do I get back

  • Three levels, and that is the budget. Workspace → section → thing. If you need a fourth, it is a tab inside the thing, not a deeper route.
  • Every page answers "where am I" without reading a word — the workspace accent, the mark, the active tab.
  • A detail page always names its way back, to the specific list you came from with its filters intact, not to a generic parent. The vault note's "All notes" link is right in form and wrong in fidelity: it drops the search you arrived from.
  • The browser back button is a first-class control. This is most of why law 5 exists. If back does not undo the last view change, the view change was written in the wrong place.
  • Never a modal for anything with content in it. Modals cannot be linked, cannot be back-buttoned, and trap on a phone. Popovers for menus; pages for content; inline expansion for detail.

Latency: what is instant, what waits

Three tiers, and every interaction is assigned to one deliberately.

TierFeelsUse forMechanism
InstantNo perceptible delayFilters, sort, tabs, expand/collapse, theme, densityClient-side, or a server round-trip the UI does not wait for.
OptimisticDone immediately, reverts loudly if it failsCheck a task, move a card, pin, snooze, toggle a preferenceuseOptimistic + server action. On failure: revert and say why.
PendingVisibly workingAnything creating a row, sending mail, hitting a third partyDisabled control with its own label — "Saving…", "Importing…". Never a full-page spinner.

Optimistic without a visible failure path is a lie. The pipeline board already does this correctly — it reverts the card and shows the error. Every optimistic write must have that second half written at the same time, or it silently drops user actions.

Undo beats confirm

A confirm dialog asks the user to predict the consequences of an action they have not seen yet. An undo lets them look at the result and change their mind. Undo is almost always the better design, and it is faster in the common case, which is the case where they meant it.

ActionPattern
Reversible, low blast radius — complete, drop, snooze, dismiss, mark returnedDo it, then offer undo in the status line for ~10 seconds.
Reversible but expensive to reverse — delete an order, remove a pursuitSoft delete + an undo, and a recoverable list in settings. orders.deleted_at already works this way and it is the right instinct.
Genuinely irreversible — delete the account, revoke a grant, disconnect a mailboxInline two-step confirm that names what will be lost. Never window.confirm.

Mechanics: useToast() from components/ui/toast.tsx for "do it, then offer undo" — bottom left, monospace, six seconds, at most two at once; <ConfirmStep> from components/ui/confirm-step.tsx for the inline two-step; and ActionMenu items with confirm arm in place inside the menu. There is no window.confirm left.

Bulk

A list where you routinely act on ten things needs a way to act on ten things. Today there is none anywhere in the app, which means clearing a triage backlog is forty individual clicks — and that is precisely why triage backlogs do not get cleared.

  • Selection appears on hover of the row's left edge, not as a permanently visible checkbox column that taxes every ordinary scan.
  • Shift-click extends a range. ⌘-click toggles one.
  • A selection turns the page header into an action bar — "3 selected · Confirm · Discard · Clear" — replacing the header rather than stacking below it.
  • Esc clears the selection. Always.
  • A bulk action gets one undo for the whole batch, not one per row.

Search is one system

There are four search implementations in the app today — a debounced client field, a plain GET form, a relevance-ranked in-memory filter, and a Postgres full-text query — each with its own behaviour and its own placeholder conventions. They should differ in backend and agree in behaviour.

  • Always a GET form underneath, enhanced with debounce, never replaced by it.
  • The query lives in ?q=, and typing uses replace so a search does not leave one history entry per keystroke between you and the page you came from.
  • Say what was searched and how. "12 notes matching 'kettle'". If results are relevance-ranked, say so and disable the sort control — the app already does this on inventory and it is exactly right.
  • Show why a row matched — an excerpt around the hit, with the term marked. A result list that does not show the match makes the user open every row to find out.
  • A search with no results is a filtered empty state, never a blank area.
  • ⌘K searches across workspaces; the page field searches within. Two scopes, two entry points, no ambiguity.

Forms: the save model

  • Pick one model per surface and make it obvious which. Settings-shaped things autosave on blur with a quiet "Saved" in the status line. Object-shaped things (an order, a role) have an explicit Save. Never mix them in one panel.
  • Validate on blur, not on keystroke. Telling someone their email is invalid while they are still typing it is scolding them for not having finished.
  • Re-validate and show every error on submit, and move focus to the first one.
  • Never lose typing. A form with unsaved changes warns before navigation; a multi-step form keeps state in the URL or in storage.
  • Server errors land on the field that caused them where possible, and in the form's banner only when they genuinely belong to the whole form.

Cross-workspace flow

This is the app's actual differentiator and it deserves its own rules. A return window closing is a shopping fact, a job follow-up is a jobs fact, and the agenda shows both without owning either. Nothing is ever copied. The todo module reads from the workspace that owns the row, at render time, and writes back to it when you act.

  • The owning workspace is always named and always reachable from the borrowed row. A row you cannot trace back to its source is a row you cannot trust.
  • Acting on a borrowed row writes to the owner, and the owner's own page shows the change immediately. There is no second state to reconcile.
  • A source that fails is named, not omitted. Law 2, and the single most important rule on any page that reads more than one schema.
  • A borrowed row is never editable in the borrower. You can act on it — snooze, complete, dismiss — but you rename it where it lives.
  • Every source is switchable off, and everything except your own list starts off. A cross-workspace feature that opts you in is a feature that floods the page you rely on.

The attention ladder

What earns an interruption, from cheapest to most expensive. Use the lowest rung that works; each rung up costs the user more and can only be spent so often.

Finding one row in five hundred

  • Sort, filter, group and search are four different questions and a list of any size needs all four. Inventory has them; nothing else does.
  • Group headers carry a subtotal — a group you cannot total is a group that only reorders the problem.
  • The default order answers the page's own question. An agenda sorts by when it runs out. An archive sorts by recency. A vault sorts by path, because a git tree carries no timestamps and sorting a mostly-null column looks like a bug.
  • Never paginate what can be virtualised, and never virtualise what fits. Pagination is a last resort: it breaks ⌘F, which is the tool people actually reach for.
  • Row density is scannable at a glance: one strong line, one supporting line, one number. A third line means the row is trying to be a detail page.

First run

  • A new account is empty on every surface, so every surface needs its empty state written before it ships. This is already policy and it is why the app survives being new.
  • Onboarding asks for exactly one thing and explains what it gets you before it asks. The Gmail pre-consent screen is the model.
  • Skip is always available and never punished. A workspace that will not open without a connected mailbox is a workspace that loses the person who wanted to try it.
  • The empty-to-full transition is a designed moment. A first sync mid-flight says so, says what it has found so far, and does not pretend to be finished.

Touch

  • Nothing depends on hover. Row actions are visible on touch and revealed on hover — opacity-100 sm:opacity-0, not the other way round.
  • Nothing depends on drag. Every drag has a menu equivalent on the same object.
  • 44px targets, or 44px of clear space around a smaller one.
  • Sheets, not popovers, below sm — pinned to the viewport, not anchored to a button that is three-quarters of the way across the screen.
  • 16px inputs, always, or iOS zooms the page on focus and the layout you designed is gone.
  • The primary action is reachable with a thumb. If the page has one obvious verb, it is not in the top-right corner on a phone.

Alive — the things a big product would not ship

Shipped: the sigil (components/ui/sigil.tsx, via EmptyState tone="finished"), the status line, hold-⌘ hints (components/shell/key-hints.tsx; ⌥1–9 open sections), the density dial. Not yet: marginalia, seams, sound.

Each of these is deliberately outside what a large team could justify. Each is here because this is one person's dashboard, and because the cost of being wrong is a setting someone switches off rather than a churned enterprise account. Each obeys law 7: chrome, never data.

1 · The quiet-day sigil

Nothing needs you today.

No interviews in the next 10 days, and nothing waiting on a reply.

A small mark, generated deterministically from hash(user_id + date), drawn in the workspace accent. It is different every day and it is yours — the same date gives the same mark forever, so it is a fact about the day rather than a random flourish. It appears in exactly one situation: the page is empty because you are finished, not because you have nothing yet.

Why it is safe: it occupies a moment that is otherwise dead space, it never appears next to data, it costs one inline SVG, and a person who hates it never sees it on a busy day anyway.

2 · The status line

A persistent one-line ticker at the bottom of the shell — what the system just did, in monospace, unprompted.

mirrored 12 notes · imported 4 orders from gmail · 2 items need review 14:22

Straight out of 1994, and genuinely the right answer for an app whose whole job is ingesting things in the background. It replaces most of what the notification bell would otherwise shout, it makes a quiet system feel alive rather than broken, and it directly serves law 2 — the app is visibly telling you what it did.

Rules: one line, never taller. Never blocks anything. Fades to ink-faint after ten seconds and stays there. Clicking it opens the full activity log. Off is a setting.

3 · Marginalia

the second kettle was a gift — don't count it against the budget

Pin a note anywhere, on any page, at a slight angle. It belongs to the page path, so it is still there next time. This is the single most "your own homepage" idea in the system, and no production tool ships it because it does not fit a data model.

Rules: rotation is at most 2°, derived from a hash of the note id so it never jitters between renders. Hard offset shadow, no blur — it is paper, not a floating panel. Never overlaps content: it lays out in a margin column and collapses to an inline block below lg.

4 · Hold ⌘ to see everything

Inventory3 Orders2 Returns4 hold ⌘ and every shortcut appears in place

Not a modal cheat-sheet listing shortcuts in the abstract — the hints appear on the actual controls, so you learn the keyboard by looking at the thing you were already about to click. Release and they vanish. This is the fastest way anyone has ever learned a keyboard-driven app, and it is almost never built because it is fiddly.

5 · The density dial

Comfortable · Snug · Dense, live in the top bar, changing the whole app's rhythm by scaling one spacing variable. Most products bury this in a settings page if they ship it at all. Here it is one click, because "how much do I want to see at once" changes with what you are doing — reading a note versus working a review queue of forty. 

/* One variable, three values. Every padding derives from it. */
[data-density="comfortable"] { --row-y: .625rem; --card-p: 1.25rem; }
[data-density="snug"]        { --row-y: .5rem;   --card-p: 1rem; }
[data-density="dense"]       { --row-y: .375rem; --card-p: .75rem; }

6 · Seams mode

A toggle that reveals where every number came from — this figure is 14 orders from two inboxes, last synced 09:12; this note was mirrored from a git tree with no timestamp, so the date you are seeing is from its frontmatter. Law 2 taken to its logical end: an app that refuses to lie by omission should be able to show its working on demand.

Off by default. When on, provenance renders as a dotted underline on any derived figure with the detail on hover and focus.

7 · Sound, off by default

One sound: a short, soft, low-volume click when something completes — an import finishes, a queue empties. Under 80ms, never on a page load, never for errors, off unless explicitly enabled. This is the riskiest item on the list and it is the reason it needs the strictest rules.

Everything on this page is a setting, and every setting defaults to the calm option. Themes, the status line, sigils, marginalia, sound, density, seams. The personality is opt-in depth, not a tax the user pays on first run. A person who turns all of it off still gets a precise, well-built, contrast-correct tool — that is the floor, and the flourishes are the ceiling.

Accessibility contract

  • 4.5:1 for text, in every theme. Non-negotiable and machine-checked.
  • Visible focus. :focus-visible is a 2px accent outline at 2px offset, on every interactive element, in every theme. Never outline: none without a replacement.
  • Every interactive element is reachable and operable from a keyboard. The pipeline board's drag is currently mouse-only; a keyboard and touch path (a status picker on the card) is required, not optional.
  • Popovers and dialogs: aria-modal, focus moved in, focus trapped, focus returned. Menus get a real menu keyboard model with roving tabindex.
  • Icon-only controls carry an accessible name — aria-label or an sr-only span. A title alone is not a name.
  • Never colour alone. A status is a badge with a word in it, not a bare dot. A star rating is "★★★" plus a label saying out of what.
  • Touch targets are 44px or have 44px of spacing around them. The 18px agenda checkbox is on the edge and gets padding rather than a bigger box.
  • aria-current="page" on every active nav item, rail item and tab.

The app talks like a knowledgeable friend who respects your time — plain, specific, unhurried, never chirpy. The existing copy is already excellent; this is a description of it, not a change to it.

  • Explain the rule when the rule is surprising. If a figure excludes something, the page says what and why, in one sentence, next to the figure.
  • Sentence case everywhere. Title Case is for proper nouns.
  • No exclamation marks. No emoji in product copy. Emoji are fine in a user's own note text; they are never in the app's voice.
  • Buttons are verbs the user is doing: "Add an order", "Clear filters", "Reconnect the vault". Never "Submit", "OK", "Confirm".
  • Numbers are formatted, always — money through lib/money.ts, dates in the account's timezone, never a raw ISO string.

Before you ship a surface

  • Every colour is a token. No raw Tailwind palette classes anywhere in the diff.
  • It has been looked at in Paper, Ink and one of Riso/Terminal/Dusk. Nothing disappears, nothing glows.
  • Every text/ground pair clears 4.5:1 in every theme.
  • Type uses named steps. No text-[Npx]. Chrome is 13px, content is 14px.
  • Numbers in a column are .tabular. Money went through lib/money.ts.
  • Containers use <Card>; banners use <Banner>; empty states use <EmptyState>.
  • The empty state branches on filtered vs unfiltered, with different copy and different actions.
  • A section with nothing in it is not rendered. No "0 of 0".
  • A source that could fail says so in place when it does.
  • Filters, search, sort and tab live in the URL and survive a refresh and the back button.
  • Search works with JavaScript disabled.
  • There is a loading.tsx rendering this page's real shape, and an error.tsx.
  • Keyboard: tab order is sane, focus is visible, every action is reachable, popovers trap and return focus.
  • Touch: actions are visible without hover; targets are 44px; nothing depends on a pointer.
  • Checked at 375px wide. Tables became lists or scroll with a visible edge. The active nav tab is on screen.
  • Any new animation is in the prefers-reduced-motion block in the same commit.
  • Any flourish is chrome, is under a second, and can be switched off.
  • Every write is assigned a latency tier — instant, optimistic or pending — and an optimistic one has its failure path written in the same commit.
  • Reversible actions offer undo rather than asking for confirmation. Only irreversible ones confirm.
  • The list can be worked from the keyboard: move, open, select, act, escape.
  • A borrowed row from another workspace names its owner and is reachable from it. Nothing is copied.
  • The interruption uses the lowest rung of the attention ladder that works.
  • Icons are Lucide at 1.75 stroke in currentColor. No emoji.
  • No gradient blends two hues.
  • Every non-obvious decision carries a comment saying why.

Never

  • Never a raw Tailwind palette colour. bg-emerald-50 is a bug in four themes.
  • Never a two-hue gradient. One hue, 20% lightness at most, or nothing.
  • Never an emoji as an interface icon.
  • Never green for spending, or for generic success.
  • Never a pipeline status hue for anything but that pipeline status.
  • Never text-ink-faint on something the user needs to read.
  • Never white text on caution-fill (2.3:1). Dark ink on the fill.
  • Never a fourth copy of the top bar, page header or left rail.
  • Never hand-write rounded-card border border-border bg-surface.
  • Never a tab, filter or sort that lives only in useState.
  • Never window.confirm or window.alert. Inline two-step confirm.
  • Never a resting drop shadow on a card.
  • Never a spinner where a skeleton of the real shape would do.
  • Never an empty section rendered as "0".
  • Never a silently shortened list when a source failed.
  • Never a flourish next to a number.
  • Never a flourish that cannot be turned off.

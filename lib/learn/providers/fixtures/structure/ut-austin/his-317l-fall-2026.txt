Course Description
“Who shall write the history of the American revolution? Who can write it? Who will ever be able to
write it?”
--John Adams, in a letter to Thomas Jefferson, 1815
This course will offer a new perspective on a series of historical events that you might think are familiar
to you – namely, the American Revolutionary War, its antecedents, and its aftermath. In this course
we will examine how the Revolution took place, what it meant to the people who lived through it, and

what it meant to the world. This will involve reading and analyzing documents from the period the
American Revolution and seeking to understand them in context. We will trace the spread of people
and ideas around the world which resulted in the American Revolution. As such, this course will help
you to understand the historical roots of changes that still impact our lives today. This is a lower-level
survey course. No prior knowledge or prerequisites are required to succeed in this course. Students
should be aware that we will be discussing topics such as colonization, enslavement, and dispossession
in their relevant historical context. In this class, you will learn how to read and engage with primary
sources, how to build historical arguments, and how to make sense of analytical arguments from
varying perspectives.
Learning Outcomes
In this course, you will develop the following skills:
• Understanding the complexities of American Revolution, its causes and consequences,
and why it mattered in the context of the eighteenth-century world and our world
today
• Learning to read and analyze primary sources
• Making historical arguments based on evidence and presenting (or articulating) your
ideas effectively.
• Practicing research and writing skills to convey nuanced ideas concisely and effectively

Course Assignments and Requirements
Participation: 5%
Our meetings will consist of a lecture and some discussion of the lectures and primary and secondary
source readings for the week. Occasionally, I will introduce primary sources to explore in workshops in
class. You will be required to attend lectures consistently and participate thoughtfully in class
discussions. Regular attendance and active, thoughtful, and informed participation in every class can
significantly boost your final grade. The teaching assistant and the professor will be taking note of who
is participating regularly in thoughtful and meaningful ways. Students who attend but do not
participate will receive a C for this portion of the grade.
Two Chronology Quizzes: 15% (7.5% each)
Study timelines of the major events of the American Revolution. During these two short in-class
chronology quizzes, I will provide you with several grouped series of four events that I will ask you to
place in chronological order. You will not need to memorize all the exact dates, but you will need to be
familiar with the sequence of events. For example, did the Battle of Lexington and Concord occur
before or after the Declaration of Independence? I will provide you with a full list of events that you
will need to know two weeks in advance of each exam.
One Book Report/Contemporary Comparison: 20%
Mid-way through the semester, we will collectively read and discuss Elizabeth Fenn’s book: Pox
Americana: The Great Smallpox Epidemic of 1775- 1782. After our discussion is concluded you will
each write and submit online, a c. 1000-1250 word essay in which you summarize and analyze Fenn’s
book, her evidence and her argument. You should then briefly describe your experience of the Covid
pandemic years and speculate what a historian of the future might learn about modern American
society by studying our response to the Covid pandemic.

Proposal for Research Paper: 5%
Students must submit a tentative project title, a clear research question and a brief one or two
paragraph description of the essay. Students will also include a bibliography that includes at least three
primary sources and five secondary sources.
Research Paper: 25%
You will choose a topic, research it and write a research paper (minimum 2,500 words and maximum
3,000 words). The paper must be based on both primary and secondary sources. It should be focused
on the interpretation and contextualization of primary sources. This paper will be due at the end of the
last meeting. I will provide a list of topics. You are strongly encouraged to meet with me to discuss the
proposed project and your sources before you begin writing.
Final Exam: 30%
This exam will be comprehensive. It may consist of identification questions, longer analytical essay
questions, historical fiction analysis, short answer questions and multiple-choice questions. It will
address all the lectures, discussions and assigned readings for the course as well as any additional in-class
handouts.
Absences: Life happens. I get it. You can be absent up to two times without it having a negative
impact on your grade. After two absences, your grade will be docked for each subsequent absence at
the professor’s discretion. If you need to be absent for an unexpected reason after two absences, please
email the professor explaining the circumstances of your absence.
Extensions: If you think you will need an extension for an assignment, please get in touch with me

Late Policy: The instructor reserves the right to dock a full letter grade per day for late

Core Requirements
U.S. History

This course may be used to fulfill three hours of the U.S. history component of the university Core
Curriculum and addresses the following four core objectives established by the Texas Higher
Education Coordinating Board: communication skills, critical thinking skills, personal responsibility,
and social responsibility.
What this means for you on a practical level is that you will build your skills in:
• Critical Thinking as assessed through a final research paper and a final exam which will
require analysis, evaluation, and synthesis of primary and secondary sources.
• Communication as assessed through a short book report/contemporary comparison paper
(1000-1250 words), a final research paper (of 2,500 to 3,000 words), in-class discussions, and
analysis of visual sources.
• Social Responsibility: We will discuss ideas of social responsibility in the seventeenth and
eighteenth centuries as compared to notions of social responsibility today (e.g., lectures on
Duels & Honor, Women at War, and Slavery and the Founders). You will be assessed through
participation in in-class discussions and through essay questions on the comprehensive final
exam.
• Personal Responsibility: You will connect choices, actions, and consequences to ethical
decision-making by adopting and defending a judgement you would make about a historical
decision based on your reading of Elizabeth Fenn's book, Pox Americana. Ethical decision-
making will also feature in our class discussions based on primary sources like George
Washington's Rules of Civility.
